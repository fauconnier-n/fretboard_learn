# Music pedagogy and motor-learning evidence for learning fretboard note locations

Scope note: These notes separate (P) peer-reviewed or empirical evidence from (T) teacher opinion / pedagogy / anecdote and (V) vendor feature claims. Peer-reviewed research specifically on guitar fretboard note learning is very thin. Most evidence is borrowed from general motor learning (laboratory tasks, sport), from clarinet and piano studies, and from cognitive psychology. Research conducted Oct 2026. About 20 tool calls were used, so some items are lighter than ideal (see Gaps).

## Q1. Common fretboard learning methods and the reasoning behind them

### Takeaway
Teachers agree on a "landmarks + low strings first + octave shapes to everything else" approach: learn the natural notes on the 6th and 5th strings (frets 0-12), anchor them to the inlay dots and the 12th-fret repeat, then reach every other string through octave shapes, with the B-string offset as the one exception. Interval-based systems (Tom Quayle/Solo) are the advanced extension. All of this is teacher opinion (T). I found no controlled study comparing these methods on guitar.

### Cited Findings
- (T) JustinGuitar (lesson IM-116): you need the notes on the 6th and 5th strings from the nut to the 12th fret before using octave shapes to name any note. Progress gets hard without at least those two strings. The goal is instant recall. — [Amara transcript of JustinGuitar IM-116](https://amara.org/de/videos/r5oCpekHUOqd/en/224180/)
- (T, anecdote) JustinGuitar community learners describe going one string at a time. They start on low E with a random-note generator calling out a few notes, add more as confidence grows, then move to the next string and notice the same notes on both. Others drill through the Circle of Fourths, or play scales with each note on a different string. — [JustinGuitar community: "Is this enough to learn the notes?"](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/is-this-enough-to-learn-the-notes-on-the-fretboard/413282); [JustinGuitar community: "How did you learn your fretboard?"](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/how-did-you-learn-your-fretboard-note-names/36003)
- (T) Octave-shape rule: from a note, go two strings over and two frets up for the octave. Crossing the B string needs three frets instead of two, because of the G–B major-third tuning offset. — [Riffhard: How to learn notes on the guitar](https://www.riffhard.com/?p=35669); [JustinGuitar community: octave shapes](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/octave-shapes-to-find-all-notes-on-the-fingerboard/118349)
- (T) Desi Serna (Fretboard Theory author; Rock Guitar For Dummies) recommends knowing just the natural notes on the 6th and 5th strings rather than every fret, because most scale patterns and chord shapes are rooted there. Octave shapes then track the notes elsewhere. — [Dummies (Serna): Getting to know the notes on a guitar fretboard](https://dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/guitar/getting-to-know-the-notes-on-a-guitar-fretboard-162886); [Dummies (Serna): Track notes with octaves](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/guitar/how-to-track-notes-and-play-songs-with-octaves-on-the-guitar-155166/)
- (T) Serna's landmarks: the first inlay past the open 6th string (3rd fret) is G, and the first marker past the 12th fret is also G. On the 5th string, C sits at the first markers in both positions (3rd/15th fret). Frets 12–24 repeat frets 0–12. — [Dummies (Serna)](https://dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/guitar/getting-to-know-the-notes-on-a-guitar-fretboard-162886)
- (T) Guitar Player's "three steps to neck knowledge" lesson is another mainstream article on fretboard memorization. I did not fetch it, so its specifics are unverified. — [Guitar Player](https://www.guitarplayer.com/technique/fretboard-memorization-three-steps-to-neck-knowledge-tab)
- (T/V) Tom Quayle's approach is intervallic. You learn "intervallic function" shapes relative to a root (a "two-point intervallic function shape system") instead of memorising many scale shapes, so shapes are built in real time while improvising. It is implemented in the Solo app, co-designed with David Beebee. — [Solo app (App Store)](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1537057699); [Ultimate Fretboard Visualization course](https://soloappcourses.teachable.com); [LickLibrary: Tom Quayle Fretboard Visualisation pt 4](https://www.licklibrary.com/learn/lessons/tom-quayle/fretboard-visualisation-part-4)
- (Research status) A guitar-education Substack says peer-reviewed research specifically on fretboard learning is thin. It points to embodied-cognition arguments (Keebler 2014; Marky 2021) that putting information directly on the instrument may reduce cognitive load compared with tab or notation. These are secondary citations I could not verify. — [The Erudite Guitarist: The science of fretboard learning](https://theeruditeguitarist.substack.com/p/the-science-of-fretboard-learning)
- (Research status) The only academic work found was theses. A Portuguese master's thesis (literature review, field research and action research) looked at elementary students' difficulties with the guitar neck. A 2022 Lund University student paper evaluated a teaching material linking theory to the fretboard. — [Univ. Évora thesis](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/handle/10174/29769); [Lund student paper](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9084263)
- (Caution) Search results included a page citing a "Dr. Sarah Hennings", a Zurich neuroscience study and a Berklee study on fretboard learning. These look unverifiable or fabricated and should NOT be used. — (observed in search results; no trustworthy URL)

### Inferences
- The app's current curriculum (low E, low frets first) matches the mainstream teacher consensus. The natural next step teachers prescribe is the 5th (A) string, then octave-shape transfer, rather than strings in order 6 to 1.
- Teacher rationale implies a curriculum order: E string, then A string, then octave shapes to D/G (two strings over, two frets up), then the B string (three frets, the exception), then high e (identical to low E). The high e string can be "free" once low E is known. The app could exploit this by testing high-e positions right after low-E mastery and expecting fast acquisition.
- Landmark frets (3, 5, 7, 9, 12) are a natural "anchor first" subset. Teaching naturals before sharps and flats is a common simplification (Serna). The app's 12-pitch-class zones include all accidentals from the start, which may be a larger initial set than teachers recommend.
- A "one note everywhere" routine (find every C in a zone or across the neck) and a "one note per string across all strings" routine are both teacher favourites. They map directly onto the app's zone concept: a zone of 6 strings x N frets where one pitch class must be found on each string.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed comparison of string-by-string, note-everywhere, octave-shape, CAGED or interval methods for guitar note learning was found.
- I did not obtain primary-source descriptions of Ted Greene's, Tommy Emmanuel's or Fretboard Theory's exact note-learning routines; the searches returned nothing usable on Greene. CAGED was not specifically researched in this pass.

## Q2. Task direction (name to position, position to name, sound to position) and multimodal encoding

### Takeaway
Existing apps routinely offer both "find the note" (name to position) and "name the note" (position to name), plus an ear mode. Music-learning theory (Gordon, Kodály) strongly favours "sound before sight" and inner hearing. Inner hearing (auditory imagery) is an empirical predictor of piano sight-reading skill. Adult absolute-pitch training works only modestly for most people, so pure sound-to-position without a reference tone is a hard task. Relative (interval) ear tasks are the more realistic target.

### Cited Findings
- (V) JustinGuitar's Note Trainer app has "Name The Note" (see a fretboard spot, say what's there) and "Find A Note" modes, plus audio feedback for ear training. — [JustinGuitar Note Trainer (BlueStacks listing)](https://www.bluestacks.com/campaign/com.justinguitar.guitarnotetrainer/pl)
- (T) Gordon's Music Learning Theory rests on "sound before sight". Audiation, the inner hearing of music not physically present, is treated as the basis of all musical behaviour. Pestalozzi, Mason and Kodály also advocated training the ear before the eye. — [College Music Symposium: Music Learning Theory in collegiate music education](https://symposium.music.org/volume-31/forum-essays-1752877059/music-learning-theory-in-collegiate-music-education); [AP School of Arts: audiation-based learning](https://ap-arts.be/en/research/audiation-based-learning)
- (T/artistic research) One artistic-research project argues that the relation between sound, meaning and naming (labelling) must be developed before notation and reading are involved, and treats pitch letter-names as "the alphabet of music". — [Research Catalogue: "What's in a name?"](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/114437/153401)
- (P, piano) Kopiez & Lee (2008): of 23 predictors, the best combination for piano sight-reading achievement was trilling speed (psychomotor speed), sight-reading expertise acquired by age 15, speed of information processing, and inner hearing (auditory imagery). Together they explained 59.6% of the variance. Kopiez & Lee (2006) found that these predictors matter more as task difficulty rises, with psychomotor and mental speed acting as a "bottleneck". — [Kopiez & Lee 2006 PDF](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/5556036/mod_resource/content/0/Artigo-KopiezLee-DynamicModelSightReading-2006.pdf); [TU Dortmund repository (Kopiez/Lee)](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/731f46c7-9397-4e48-b4af-78c608553d99/content)
- (P) Adult absolute-pitch learning: Van Hedger et al. (2015, *Cognition*) showed that auditory working-memory capacity predicts how well adults learn absolute-pitch note categories. — [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/acquiring-perfect-pitch-may-be-possible-some-adults)
- (P) Van Hedger et al. (2019, *PLoS ONE*, "Absolute pitch can be learned by some adults"): explicit training led some adults to absolute-pitch performance indistinguishable from early-acquired AP, but most participants improved only modestly. Near-perfect AP may be reachable only by a subset of adults. — [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/325050v2.full); [PLoS ONE via prolekare](https://www.prolekare.cz/casopisy/plos-one/_tmp_80e3fcdedee100003f6ee73a7e7fe7da/absolute-pitch-can-be-learned-by-some-adults-114664?hl=en)
- (V) FredsBoard (iOS) has a mic-based "find and pluck" mode plus an ear-training mode that identifies notes by sound alone. Super Guitar Fretboard combines ear training, sight reading and fretboard note/chord practice. — [FredsBoard](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6757344687); [Super Guitar Fretboard Lite](https://apps.apple.com/app/id423720985)

### Inferences
- The app currently trains only name to position. Adding position to name (highlight a fret, the learner says or selects the name) would cover the reverse retrieval route. Recognition while playing and reading tab or chord charts needs position to name, which is a different retrieval direction than finding a named note. This is an inference from cognitive-psychology retrieval-practice principles; no guitar-specific study was found.
- Because the app has pitch detection, a "sound to position" mode is feasible. Given the AP evidence, it should be framed relatively: play a reference note (for example the zone's root or an open string), then play a target tone and have the learner find it. Or ask the learner to play an interval from a given note ("play a major 3rd above this C"). Absolute naming of an isolated tone should be optional and advanced.
- "Say the note name aloud as you play it" is a zero-cost multimodal add-on teachers advocate. Speech recognition is optional; a prompt is enough. A "sing then play" mode (sing the target pitch, detected by the same pitch tracker, then play it) would bring audiation and inner hearing into the loop, and inner hearing is an empirically supported predictor of reading skill (piano).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on whether saying or singing note names while learning instrument locations improves retention (the "production effect" was not verified for music in this pass).
- No study found comparing name-to-position vs position-to-name training transfer on guitar or piano.

## Q3. Contextual interference, variable practice, implicit/errorless learning, and feedback (guidance hypothesis)

### Takeaway
Random or interleaved practice tends to hurt performance during practice but helps retention and transfer. The effect is robust in labs, smaller in applied settings and weak in children/novices. Music studies are small but mostly favourable (Stambaugh 2011; Carter & Grahn 2016). The guidance hypothesis says 100% feedback or guidance breeds dependence, but replication is mixed. Self-controlled feedback has meta-analytic support for retention and transfer. Errorless (easy-to-hard) progressions can promote robust, implicit learning.

### Cited Findings
- (P, meta-analysis) Brady (2004): first meta-analysis of the contextual interference (CI) effect in motor learning, 61 studies and 139 effect sizes. Random practice impairs acquisition but improves retention and transfer relative to blocked practice. Effects were larger in basic or lab research (ES about .57) than in applied research (about .19), and larger in adults (.50) than in children (.10). The overall mean is reported around .38 (decimal not fully confirmed from the snippet). — [Brady 2004 PDF (gwern mirror)](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- (P) Later updates: Ammar et al. (2023, 2024) meta-analyses update Brady. A Frontiers 2024 systematic review re-examines CI. Jiménez Díaz et al. (2016) reported that blocked groups improved in acquisition but declined in retention. — [PMC11237090](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11237090/); [Frontiers 2024 systematic review](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1377122/full); [UCR meta-analysis on CI](https://archivo.revistas.ucr.ac.cr/index.php/pem/article/view/23830)
- (P, music, clarinet beginners) Stambaugh (2011, *JRME* 58(4):368–383): N = 41 beginning clarinetists practised three seven-pitch exercises in blocked vs random order over three sessions plus a retention session. There was no group difference at the end of practice. At retention, the random group played faster while the blocked group's speed dropped. No differences in accuracy, temporal evenness, transfer or attitudes. — [Georgia Southern Digital Commons: "When Repetition Isn't the Best Practice Strategy"](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/music-facpubs/2)
- (P, music, school) A separate JRME study (7th-grade clarinet and saxophone) found no main effects of practice schedule on technical accuracy, attitude or musicality at immediate or 24-hour testing. — [Georgia Southern: differing levels of CI](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/music-facpubs/13); [RIC Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.ric.edu/facultypublications/647)
- (P, music, advanced clarinet) Carter & Grahn (2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251): 10 advanced clarinetists. Blocked = 12 min per piece; interleaved = 3-minute chunks alternating to 12 min per piece. Interleaved pieces were generally rated higher by three professional judges, though the raters did not agree uniformly. Participants said interleaving helped goal setting, focus and spotting mistakes. The sample was small, so findings are preliminary. — [PMC4989027](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4989027/)
- (P) Guidance hypothesis (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984): frequent augmented feedback makes learners depend on it instead of intrinsic feedback. Reduced frequency encourages self-processing. Supported by Wulf & Schmidt 1989, Winstein & Schmidt 1990, Nicholson & Schmidt 1991 and Weeks & Kordus 1998. Several replications failed (Sparrow & Summers 1992; Wishart & Lee 1997; Lai & Shea 1998; Wulf et al. 1998), and Wulf & Shea (2004) concluded there is little evidence that feedback after every trial creates dependence. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2010 (fpsyg.2010.00190)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00190/full); [Marschall, Bund & Wiemeyer 2007](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/4589/1/Publikation_Marschall_Bund_Wiemeyer_2007_EJournal%20Bewegung%20und%20Training.PDF)
- (P) Self-controlled feedback: Chiviacowsky & Wulf (2002) found learners who chose when to get feedback mostly asked after trials they thought were good, and benefited. Replicated by Chiviacowsky et al. 2008, Patterson & Carter 2010 and Patterson et al. 2011. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2012 (fpsyg.2012.00338)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00338/pdf)
- (P, meta-analysis) Wang, Tao, Yuan & Guo (2025, *Behavioral Sciences*): 29 studies, 1,147 participants. Self-controlled feedback gave no acquisition advantage but improved retention and transfer. Evidence is described as mixed. Carter & Ste-Marie (2017) found an activity inserted during the feedback delay removes the advantage. — [PMC12467369](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12467369/); [MDPI Behavioral Sciences](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/9/1291)
- (P) Errorless learning: Maxwell, Masters, Kerr & Weedon (2001, *QJEP-A*), golf putting. Reducing errors during learning (an easy-to-hard progression) led to implicit learning: fewer verbalisable rules and robust performance under dual-task load. Errorful learners used explicit hypothesis testing and broke down under a secondary task. — [Univ. Birmingham research portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/the-implicit-benefit-of-learning-without-errors); [HKU hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/87930)
- (P) Mixed replication: a fine-motor (disc-propelling) replication found no protocol effects in acquisition, retention or transfer. A children's study found errorless (near-to-far) learners made fewer errors and performed better under dual-task conditions. — [JEMS: Does "errorless" practice facilitate fine motor-skill learning?](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/231); [JSMDL: Errorless practice in children](https://jsmdl.ut.ac.ir/article_69916_en.html)

### Inferences
- Interleaving: the app's spaced repetition per position is already a form of random practice. The CI evidence argues for mixing strings and zones once each zone is partially learned, rather than finishing one string fully before touching others. Pure blocked runs may make in-session accuracy look better than retention really is. Brady's finding of small effects in children and novices suggests a short blocked introduction (a few items) followed by a fast move to random order. In practice: blocked for the first exposure to a new zone, then interleave it with previously learned zones.
- Use delayed retention tests as the true progress metric (first-attempt accuracy and latency at the start of the next session), not in-session accuracy, because CI and guidance effects specifically dissociate acquisition from retention.
- The guidance hypothesis applied to the zone highlight and hints: the always-visible zone is a guidance aid. A sensible design is fading. Show the zone for new items, then shrink it, show it only after a delay or on request, and eventually remove it ("find any G on strings 4–6, frets 0–12"). The evidence for feedback dependence is mixed (Wulf & Shea 2004), so correct/incorrect feedback after every trial is probably fine. The bigger risk is the persistent spatial guidance.
- Self-controlled practice: let the learner choose when to reveal a hint or the answer, and possibly which zone to drill. Meta-analytic support covers retention and transfer.
- Errorless learning: progressive difficulty (small zone, naturals, landmarks first, then larger zones) fits the errorless or easy-to-hard protocol. That literature suggests it yields more robust, automatic retrieval under dual-task load, such as playing while listening to a band.

### Gaps
- No CI study on note-location learning on guitar or keyboard was found. The music CI studies use short technical passages, not note-name mapping.
- I did not verify the original Shea & Morgan (1979) or Magill & Hall (1990) papers directly in this pass, though they are the canonical CI origin and review respectively. Brady (2004) is the citable synthesis.
- I found no study of "fading guidance" (shrinking visual zones) in instrument learning specifically.

## Q4. Note-reading fluency / speed drills / automaticity

### Takeaway
Pedagogy material treats timed flashcard drills (shuffled, starting small and growing) as the standard way to automate note-to-key mapping, but I found no controlled study of flashcards or note-reading apps for piano or guitar. Empirical sight-reading research highlights processing speed, psychomotor speed, pattern chunking and inner hearing, which suggests speed (latency) is a meaningful target alongside accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- (T) Brent Hugh's piano pedagogy handout (Missouri Western): flashcards build a strong link between the written note and its keyboard location. Shuffle every time so students can't rely on context or memory, and start with 2–3 cards, adding more as each is mastered. — [brenthugh.com: Teaching note reading with flashcards](https://brenthugh.com/piano/flashcardteaching.html)
- (P, review) A 2025 review says accomplished sight-readers read about six or seven notes ahead versus two or three for novices, and that experts recognise stored patterns instead of reading each note individually. — [Univ. Transilvania Brașov Bulletin (Series VIII)](https://webbut.unitbv.ro/index.php/Series_VIII/article/download/11306/6735/20649)
- (P, piano) Speed of information processing and psychomotor speed act as bottlenecks for sight-reading, alongside practice-related expertise and inner hearing (Kopiez & Lee 2006/2008). — [Kopiez & Lee 2006 PDF](https://edisciplinas.usp.br/pluginfile.php/5556036/mod_resource/content/0/Artigo-KopiezLee-DynamicModelSightReading-2006.pdf)
- (P, non-music analogy) A Swedish dissertation on children's reading fluency found training studies often failed to increase reading rate even when accuracy problems were fixed. Accuracy drills alone may not build speed. — [avhandlingar.se](https://www.avhandlingar.se/om/Maj-Gun/)
- (Weak evidence) A 2024 one-group pre-experimental study (n = 16 college music students) reported fewer sight-reading errors after a 14-day Brain Gym warm-up. There was no control group, so it is uninformative. — [jurnal.unai.edu](https://jurnal.unai.edu/index.php/isc/article/download/3417/2490)
- (V) Note Trainer (Windows/Microsoft Store) markets itself as a flashcard replacement that increases speed and difficulty over time. This is a vendor claim. — [Microsoft Store listing](https://apps.microsoft.com/detail/9wzdncrdnnw5?hl=en-US)

### Inferences
- The app should track response latency per position, not just correctness, and schedule spaced repetition on "slow-correct" items as well as errors. Automaticity, which is what teachers call "instant recall" (JustinGuitar), is a speed criterion. A possible mastery rule: correct and under a time threshold (for example about 2 s) on N consecutive spaced reviews.
- Pattern chunking in sight-reading suggests that moving from single notes to short note groups (two-note intervals, triads, short melodic cells) may be the route from isolated naming to reading-like fluency.

### Gaps
- No RCT on flashcard or app-based note-reading training for piano or guitar was found.
- No guitar-specific sight-reading predictor study was found; Kopiez & Lee is piano only.

## Q5. Visual landmarks, spatial cognition, string-to-string transfer and octave relationships

### Takeaway
Teacher pedagogy relies on fixed landmarks (inlay dots at 3/5/7/9/12, the 12-fret repeat), the low E = high e identity, and octave shapes with the B-string offset. These are structural facts of standard tuning that an app can exploit for transfer. No empirical spatial-cognition study of the fretboard was found.

### Cited Findings
- (T/structural) G is at the first inlay past the open 6th string and again at the first marker past fret 12. C is at the first markers on the 5th string. The neck repeats after fret 12. — [Dummies (Serna)](https://dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/instruments/guitar/getting-to-know-the-notes-on-a-guitar-fretboard-162886)
- (T/structural) Octave shape: two strings over, two frets up, plus one extra fret when the shape crosses the B string. — [Riffhard](https://www.riffhard.com/?p=35669)
- (T) JustinGuitar: once the 6th and 5th strings are known, octave shapes reach every note on the neck. — [Amara: JustinGuitar IM-116](https://amara.org/de/videos/r5oCpekHUOqd/en/224180/)
- (T/anecdote) Learners report "noticing the same notes" on the next string after learning one string, which suggests transfer through relational patterns. — [JustinGuitar community](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/is-this-enough-to-learn-the-notes-on-the-fretboard/413282)
- (P-adjacent, unverified secondary) Embodied cognition arguments (Keebler 2014) suggest learning located on the instrument reduces the translation step. — [The Erudite Guitarist](https://theeruditeguitarist.substack.com/p/the-science-of-fretboard-learning)

### Inferences
- App ideas that use structure:
  - (a) "Mirror" items: after a low-E position is mastered, seed the high-e equivalent with an elevated starting ease factor, and test whether learning is faster.
  - (b) Octave-pair drills: show a known note and ask for its octave on another string, so learners practise the shape rule explicitly. Include the B-string exception as a specific item type.
  - (c) Landmark-first ordering: frets 0, 3, 5, 7, 12 within each zone before the in-between frets.
  - (d) An optional dot-marker display in the on-screen fretboard that mirrors the learner's real guitar. The dots are a legitimate long-term cue, unlike the zone highlight, which should fade.
- Zones that are boxes of 2–4 adjacent strings with 12 pitch classes once each are effectively position-playing boxes. That matches CAGED-like or position thinking and is good for connecting note names to the shapes players actually use.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of visuospatial memory for fretboard positions, landmark effects, or cross-string transfer was found.
- The G–B major-third offset is documented as a teaching rule, but no evidence was found on whether it causes measurably more errors. The app's own data could answer this.

## Q6. Musical context (scales, arpeggios, chord tones, melodies) vs isolated note naming; transfer to real playing

### Takeaway
Advanced teacher systems (Tom Quayle/Solo) and many learners move from isolated note-finding to intervals, scales and chord tones over progressions. The rationale is that real playing requires relational knowledge. Motor-learning CI/transfer evidence and sight-reading chunking research support adding varied, contextual tasks. No study directly tests whether isolated fretboard naming transfers to improvisation or reading.

### Cited Findings
- (V/T) Solo (Tom Quayle and David Beebee) has four trainers. A Note Trainer gives random roots and waits until you play the correct note. An Interval Trainer drills intervallic functions against a fixed or random root, with custom groups or intervals drawn from a chord or scale. A Changes Trainer covers 100+ progressions across 50 interval-function levels, with random transposition and voice-leading through single-note lines. A Scale Trainer covers 50+ scales and modes with melodic sequences starting from different chord tones. It listens to a real instrument (audio-interface input, calibration) and advances only on the correct note. The listing does not mention spaced repetition or statistics, and a reviewer requested stats. — [Solo app (App Store)](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1537057699)
- (T/anecdote) Learners combine note drilling with the Circle of Fourths and with scales played one note per string. — [JustinGuitar community](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/is-this-enough-to-learn-the-notes-on-the-fretboard/413282)
- (P) The CI and variable-practice meta-analytic findings (Brady 2004) are specifically about better transfer from varied or random practice. — [Brady 2004](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2004-brady.pdf)
- (P, music) Carter & Grahn (2016): interleaving musical excerpts improved rated performance and self-reported error-spotting. — [PMC4989027](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4989027/)
- (P, review) Expert sight-readers recognise stored patterns rather than single notes. — [Unitbv 2025 review](https://webbut.unitbv.ro/index.php/Series_VIII/article/download/11306/6735/20649)

### Inferences
- A sensible progression for the app goes in four stages:
  1. Isolated name-to-position (current).
  2. Position-to-name and "all instances of X in a zone".
  3. Relational tasks such as octaves, intervals from a given note, and the chord tones (R-3-5) of a named triad inside the zone.
  4. Contextual sequences: play the C major scale or a short melody by note names within the zone, or play the chord tones of each chord in a progression (Solo-style) with pitch detection checking each note.
  Stage 3–4 tasks use the same detection engine and turn isolated facts into the usable relational knowledge teachers say real playing needs.
- Since transfer is the open question, the app could include a "transfer probe": an occasional untrained format, such as a new zone, a short melody, or note names presented in a different order. Measuring it would show whether isolated drilling generalises for that learner.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures transfer from isolated fretboard note naming to improvisation, reading or ensemble playing.

## Appendix-style notes on existing apps (kept within Q6 scope as feature comparisons)

### Inferences
- Differentiators this app could have, given it already has exact-pitch detection via DI (which is more reliable than mic apps) and spaced repetition per position:
  - Latency-based mastery.
  - Fading zone guidance.
  - Reverse (position-to-name) and relational (interval/octave/chord-tone) tasks verified by pitch.
  - Sing-then-play.
  - Mirror-string seeding.
  - Retention-based progress reports.
  Solo lacks spaced repetition and statistics according to its listing, and FredsBoard's zone concept is the closest competitor feature.
